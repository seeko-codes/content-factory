# eigenchris: observed execution

The contrast in this pair is large enough that a single style label would be misleading. [What is Quantum Mechanics?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S5FG8zZ7hlU) explicitly identifies itself as a joke video. Its white field holds loosely hand-drawn stick figures, expressive eyes, pale gray objects and occasional red, blue, green or yellow accents. [Spinors for Beginners 25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3a4xpUtFVtU) is a long white-slide derivation with dense matrix algebra, colored components, red annotations, separators and summary tables. The cartoon is animated illustration about the experience of the subject; the second is technical lecture material. Neither sampled clip is an eligible primarily explanatory math-animation reference under the tightened scope.

The cartoon makes a student's facial expression and encounters the main visual objects. Early shots enlarge a smiling head, place a teacher at a board, and use a gray vignette around a distressed face. Calendars and paper piles represent elapsed work or effort. Later panels introduce magnets, costume-like combat figures, ghostly historical-looking people, a rocket/moon and a money throne. Those drawings are narrative/comedic metaphors. They do not encode a derivation of quantum mechanics and should not be promoted into a physics visualization just because EPR lettering or magnets are visible. Subtitle excerpts corroborate the explicitly comic social-confusion premise rather than a theorem explanation.

The spinor lecture has a pedagogical staircase from physical motivation through spinors, Clifford algebras, Lie groups/algebras and particle physics. It then distinguishes Klein-Gordon, Proca and Dirac equations in a structured comparison. Later work tracks chiral and mass bases, component equations, Euler-Lagrange manipulations, stationary solutions and boosts. Black supplies most algebra; cyan, magenta and green distinguish components or variables, while red boxes, arrows and underlining identify a step. A spacetime diagram with yellow wavefronts and a Minkowski-triangle comparison are genuine geometric support, but they occupy a small portion of an algebra-led slide lesson.

The long lecture uses strong panel divisions and side calculations to keep old and new expressions visible simultaneously. That is valuable for algebraic reference, although its density is unsuited to direct portrait cropping. A late table preserves several solution forms at once. The viewer needs basis labels and assumptions, including the stationary/rest simplification; otherwise the table could look like a universal formula detached from its setup. No presenter inset appears in the sampled lecture frames. No persistent speech-caption system is visible in either sampled set.

The cartoon ending becomes another joke panel rather than a technical summary. The lecture ending explicitly previews helicity, Dirac current, charge conjugation and matter/antimatter. Audio was not auditioned, so voice quality, comic timing and exact synchronization remain unknown. The archived pair is useful evidence of a creator's unusually wide range, not a template to average together. What ruins these executions is taking the cartoon literally as a scientific model, or simplifying the lecture by removing basis and metric conventions while retaining its formulas. A further channel search could locate eligible mathematical animations, but these samples alone do not establish them.
