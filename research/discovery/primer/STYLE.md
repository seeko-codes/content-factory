## Composition and color
A dark charcoal stage holds rounded, softly shaded creatures, fruit trees, and a shallow oblique green simulation board. These are deliberately simplified creatures rather than anatomical animals. The Short stacks a large percentage chart above a tree encounter and miniature world. The long-form opening spreads its reward matrix, examples, chart and world across the landscape frame. Their different layouts are worth keeping separate.

Rock is magenta-red, paper is bright yellow and scissors is muted cyan-blue. The color remains attached to both the creature and its chart category, while explicit labels support reading. Green foliage and orange fruit give the encounter a recognizable resource. The nearly empty background allows quite small simulated objects to remain legible. The floor is a bounded model world, not a realistic ecosystem.

## Apparatus, type and materials
The creatures have glossy or softly lit surfaces, simple dark eyes and asymmetrical silhouettes. Trees are much more visually intricate than the creatures, while bars and text remain flat. A serif-like face is visible in the questions, category labels and large closing invitation. Percentages are white and sit directly above their colored bars. ## Hook, explanatory operation and motion
The hook starts with two creatures contesting one resource. Reproduction is expressed through smaller versions of the winning creature; a local rule becomes a population mechanism without first presenting an equation. The question then lists competing outcomes before running the model. Bar heights and percentage values change in place while the miniature world remains an explanatory anchor. These frame samples establish changing state and layout persistence; they do not measure acceleration, frame cadence or exact timing curves.

The long-form opening adds the directional three-way dominance diagram and a payoff matrix. It also briefly introduces lizard images to connect the toy model to an example from nature. That is a distinct evidence register from the blobs. A single simulation outcome is an example, not a theorem about inevitable dominance.
