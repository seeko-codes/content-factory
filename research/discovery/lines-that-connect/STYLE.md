# Lines That Connect: observed execution

The two reviewed videos combine a very restrained black/white mathematical page with occasional full, colorful graph surfaces. [How to Take the Factorial of Any Number](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v_HeaeUUOnc) begins in a visibly real Desmos interface, then develops its argument through centered serif formulas and yellow/cyan graph constructions. [Extending the Riemann Zeta Function with Fractional Sums](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=N0Ho0XGuXZs) starts with a conventional series expression, expands into a color-mapped complex plane, then returns to exact symbolic identities. The variation is purposeful: the complex-plane imagery belongs to the function being explained, rather than serving as an all-purpose background.

The creator's own [site](https://linesthatconnect.github.io/) identifies Michael and says he uses Manim for nearly all animations, Audacity/Rode NT1 for voice recording, DaVinci Resolve for editing and FL Studio for some music and voice processing. That is first-party workflow evidence, separate from what the frames show. The apparent serif mathematical font and economical placement are observed, but exact font files and motion curves are not. No face-camera inset appears in the sampled pair. No full-sentence karaoke captions occupy the lower screen; text is mostly equations, short section labels, careful qualifiers and references.

The factorial video deliberately exposes a gap between a familiar rule and a satisfying derivation. Around 02:39 the gamma integral is accompanied by an easy-to-prove/hard-to-derive contrast. That is a precise epistemic role for typography: the issue is not whether an expression can be checked, but how someone might arrive at it. Later, sampled data points and a yellow curve compare discrete factorial behavior to a continuous candidate. Black space lets the symbols remain the main object even when the proof becomes dense.

The zeta video uses domain coloring with visible axes and mesh curves. A panel initially marks the half-plane where the ordinary series converges; later, a full colored continuation includes an explicit pole exception. It also displays negative-one/twelfth notation, but surrounds it with different definitions and formulas. Any adaptation must preserve the difference between ordinary summation and analytic continuation, since the colorful function plot alone cannot authorize a divergent-series equality. The video's long equations have readable horizontal room in landscape; a vertical crop would destroy that arrangement.

Both samples end on nested black-and-white polygons, giving a striking shared geometric signature after the formal material. The preceding states differ: the factorial video collects related identities, while the zeta video points to resources. The execution is useful for a derivation that must alternate discrete data, a continuous extension and a formal characterization. It would fail if screenshots of software replaced the actual mathematical comparison, if the colored plane lost axes and domain conditions, or if the final elegant expression were presented without the uniqueness or convergence issue that made finding it interesting.

## Evidence boundary

These are two-video, sixteen-frame-per-video sampled observations, not continuous playback. Intermediate motion is inferred only where successive states establish it. Exact timing curves, frame-accurate transitions, font identities and sound quality were not verified. Captions mentioned below are visible authored text; available subtitle text was used only to corroborate terms. The pair is intentionally contrasting and does not define every upload.
