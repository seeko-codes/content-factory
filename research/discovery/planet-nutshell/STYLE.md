# Planet Nutshell: sampled mathematical animation

Observed 2026-10-29. Scope: the two linked videos in [breakdowns.md](breakdowns.md). Broader research preserved outside the final portrait-short core.

## Curriculum cartoons with a mathematical task

The two sampled Math Shorts are produced educational cartoons, not short extracts of a classroom. A pale graph-paper opening and closing establish the series. The main lesson uses large flat colored fields, simplified characters and thick, clean shapes. Cyan, purple, orange and yellow make object identity easy to preserve from one stage to another. Shadows and selected perspective cues imply solids without attempting realistic CGI. Diagram labels use clear upright lettering; exact font and production software were not verified.

Slicing Three Dimensional Figures uses a cartoon ninja as the agent who cuts a cylinder and a square pyramid. A cyan sword, yellow cutting plane and yellow exposed faces make the operation concrete. The surrounding field is a warm orange. The solids stay recognizable as the cut is introduced, the pieces separate and the section is displayed. The scene later places the cut beside a coordinate plane, separating the three-dimensional source object from the two-dimensional shape the viewer should name. The point is not only that something has been sliced: the reveal identifies what the slice looks like when viewed face on.

Rotation uses a different chain of familiar images. A tilted cat poster and a cyclist's wheel introduce turning before a coordinate triangle appears. A turquoise graph background replaces the everyday illustration. Orange triangles, a labeled origin, dashed paths and image labels organize the transformation. The origin gets its own close attention, making the center of rotation explicit rather than letting the triangle simply jump to a different quadrant. This is a coordinate-transformation variant, not another solid-cutting lesson.

## How the explanation is carried

Characters and props create the hook, but the mathematical diagrams eventually take over the screen. The ninja supplies a physical reason for a cutting plane; a wheel supplies a familiar rotation before an abstract origin is named. The humor is source specific and visually observable. A precise voice characterization or musical rhythm is not established because the audio was not independently heard. Automatic English captions were read for Slicing; Rotation remains visual only after a public subtitle request returned HTTP 429. No access bypass was attempted.

Titles, coordinate labels and short mathematical terms are placed next to their objects. The inspected frames do not show a continuous large speech-caption track. This leaves room for figures, though coordinates can still become small when a complete grid is shown. Slicing's stable object colors help a viewer connect the original solid to the new yellow section. Rotation's original and image triangles can coexist, allowing comparison of position and orientation. The latter would be weakened by replacing both with a single unexplained final triangle.

Both videos return to a pale graph-paper Math Shorts logo with Planet Nutshell and UEN marks. Slicing includes a late recap showing the two cut solids before that closing card. Rotation instead revisits the cyclist late in the sample. These are distinct endings within a common series package. The title Math Shorts refers to an educational series from 2013-2014; both uploads are landscape and longer than a modern one-minute phone reel.

## Use and precise limits

Use the slicing treatment to distinguish the solid, the cutting plane and the resulting plane figure. Use the rotation treatment when an everyday turning action can prepare a coordinate transformation. A version that keeps only the funny character and removes the plane orientation, center or labels would lose the explanation. The geometry must also remain conditional. A plane perpendicular to a pyramid's base does not always make the same cross-section: a triangular slice requires an appropriate position, including the apex in the illustrated case. A generic statement that every perpendicular cut of any square pyramid is a triangle would overgeneralize the example.

The twelve-frame review per clip supports layout, colors, visible correspondence and broad reveal order. It cannot verify every in-between transformation, exact cut durations, sound cues or easing. These cartoons are useful references for concretizing geometry with purposeful actions. They are not evidence that every everyday analogy proves a theorem, nor that the current mixed-topic channel's whole catalogue belongs in a math-animation gallery. Only the sampled mathematical series is included here.
