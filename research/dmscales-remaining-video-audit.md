## Method and limits

Used the installed `watch` skill to obtain public media and English automatic subtitles without authentication or cookies. Read both timestamped transcripts. Inspected all 24 selected scene frames per video, then targeted frames at opening, middle, and closing moments: **35 frames for Knowledge, 40 for Idea, plus both thumbnails**. Scene threshold was 0.30. Targeted opening samples include 0, 3, 8, 15, and 30 seconds. Knowledge's sparse sample across 16 minutes does not resolve every edit; Idea's selected frames often land inside zooms or visually distorted transitions, so readable stills received more weight. Review was sampled, not continuous playback. Audio observations concern the transcript, not independently assessed vocal delivery or sound design.

Only original analysis is retained here. Downloaded media, subtitles, thumbnails, frames, and intermediate files were temporary and removed. This report verifies what the videos say and show; it does not establish the truth of their platform-policy, income, algorithm, audience, or business claims.

## Idea: research the angle, then account for the promise

The thumbnail fills the frame with a channel graph, a pointer at its rise, and the possibility of a single breakthrough upload. The title and first speech reinforce the same outcome. The visible graph records a displayed trajectory, not proof that every channel is one upload away from comparable growth.

- [00:00:00-00:00:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=0s): a contrarian promise dismisses the need for months of persistence and promises a concise, freely shared method. Sampled captions emphasize the challenge and ease. Teaching begins around 00:00:16. This is another explicit exception to an all-channel claim of immediate teaching.
- [00:00:28-00:01:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=28s): a crowd drawing and feed examples establish a viewer choosing among alternatives. By the 60-second sample, several creator figures surround one audience group. The diagram maps competition to competing offers instead of merely restating the voice.
- [00:01:12-00:01:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=72s): he compares two videos about ancient daily life and attributes their different performance to the angle. The 75-second frame also acknowledges that one channel is larger. This is a useful observed caution inside the example: different titles, channels, and distribution histories prevent a causal title comparison.
- [00:01:44-00:02:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=104s): the creator describes finding a gap for concise algorithm explanations instead of another general growth guide. At [00:02:24-00:02:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=144s), he explains his own thumbnail and title decisions, then admits the easy-success framing says what he believes his audience wants to hear even though the work is not actually easy. This materially challenges any inference that these reference promises should be copied uncritically.
- [00:02:37-00:03:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=157s): niche viewing, saved references, tools, and repeated angle exploration lead into a visible list of candidate titles around 00:03:18. He rejects a psychology framing because he believes it would attract a smaller, more systematic audience. The list is concrete evidence of considering alternatives, not proof his chosen audience diagnosis is correct.
- [00:03:36-00:04:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=216s): he labels the Knowledge video a poor result and attributes that to its time commitment and audience motivation. At the 220-second sample its screenshot shows about 34k views at 12 days; the later audit snapshot is about 99k at 166 days. This illustrates why evaluation age matters. His suggestion to inspect competitors' whole catalogues, including weak performers, is stronger research practice than collecting only winners. His regular/casual/new audience categories are conceptual viewer groups here; do not equate them automatically with identically named analytics fields.
- [00:04:38-00:05:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VQvIV__toUk&t=278s): he argues for delaying the main answer, refining every edit, and showing drafts to people. Rapid zoom/distortion transitions and changing examples are prominent in sampled frames. Finally he rejects a guaranteed title/thumbnail formula. The ending points to Discord and a playlist, with no single worked ideation exercise proving the method on a new case.

**Interpretation:** the strongest transferable move is considering several ways to frame the same useful material for a named audience. The aggressive transitions are a separate execution choice. This newer, heavily edited upload has fewer snapshot views than the simpler original algorithm videos; age, topic, and distribution prevent attributing that difference to editing.

## Changes recommended for the principles and the next twenty videos

These are production judgments informed by the full catalogue, not experimentally established platform rules.

1. **Keep promise and payoff linked, but qualify the observational claim.** Immediate teaching characterizes the three algorithm explainers, while both remaining videos have promotional preambles. Our preference for delivering a useful first step early is a teaching decision, not a universal habit of this creator.
2. **Add an angle decision before committing a slate row.** For the same mathematical insight, draft a few distinct viewer-facing questions. Choose the one tied to a real student difficulty, then ensure the first frame and proof fulfill it. For example, a slope row could answer how to read a steepness, why two triangles give the same ratio, or how a tempting inverted ratio fails. Those are different promises and should receive different scripts.
3. **Study misses alongside winners.** Include recent and lower-performing references with comparable topic, age, and format when prioritizing the twenty. Record why a question matters to the intended SAT student separately from why a reference attracted views. Do not allow a public-view ranking alone to determine curricular value.
4. **Retain early partial payoffs.** The creator advocates keeping the answer late, but teaching can deliver useful progress throughout: solve one step, reveal its limitation, then resolve the deeper question. Reserve the final synthesis or fresh application for the end. Withholding a simple answer artificially would weaken the honest-promise standard.
5. **Keep relevant motion as an authored standard.** The full catalogue contains gameplay, memes, and aggressive transitions as well as graphs and diagrams. The channel does not establish that every visible change should explain something. Our choice to make motion carry the mathematics remains supported by the learning evidence already cited in [LOGIC.md](../LOGIC.md), rather than by universal imitation of these videos.
6. **Make observed learning the review criterion.** The creator's suggestion to show drafts and observe reactions is compatible with our review process. For each proposed reel, ask a target student what was promised, what changed, and whether they can solve a nearby example. Do not replace those checks with the editor's confidence that an angle will go viral.

The existing six-rule synthesis largely survives. Its factual scope should expand from three sampled uploads to all five, and its distinction between reference execution, inferred mechanisms, and our own educational standards should become more explicit. The channel's self-analysis is useful evidence of its author's decisions, not independent verification of those decisions' effectiveness.
