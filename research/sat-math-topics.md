# SAT math: video topics

Historical research, preserved for lineage. The [fresh Ubilum study](channels/ubilumapp/STYLE.md) supersedes the performance interpretations below; its [metric audit](channels/ubilumapp/ubilumapp-performance.md) verifies icon associations and comparison limits. The active slate is [v2](today-2026-10-29-v2.md). Old causal claims and expected reach priorities are hypotheses, not production authority.

This is research only. It lists the big ideas in SAT math, prioritized by the formats that perform best on the reference channel, that become "wait, *that's* why?" moments when you see them, each sized for one 60-second video in this style. Format, packaging and production decisions are out of scope here.

Each topic has four parts:
- **Hook:** the opening line.
- **Insight:** the one thing that clicks.
- **Picture:** what the viewer watches.
- **On the SAT:** the question types it unlocks.

Topics are grouped by the four digital SAT math domains, using the College Board's own skill names. The approximate share of the math section is in brackets ([skills insight](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/skills-insight-digital-sat-suite.pdf), [teacher guide](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org:443/media/pdf/redesigned-sat-k12-teacher-implementation-guide.pdf)).

**What separates the winners:**

2. **One fact everyone memorized, shown transforming.** "Why does sine turn into cosine?" is the top single-concept post. The fact is famous and memorized but not understood, and the answer is one continuous motion that makes it obvious.
3. **Both 3D and 2D transformations appear among the leaders.** The gradient on a moving 3D surface got 5,870 likes; the integral on a flat speed chart got 922. These are different topics and posts, so their counts do not establish a 3D advantage. The 2D sine-to-cosine explainer has 15.1K likes. Borrow clear visual correspondence and choose dimensionality for the idea. See the [timestamped asset audit](reference-asset-audit.md).
4. **What falls flat: no personal stake.** Research problems (Millennium series), abstract identities (e^x) and narrow symbol definitions do 5-50 times worse. The viewer isn't taking a course in them and isn't scared of them.

Caveats: this is nine posts with visible counts, posted on different dates, so older posts have had longer to accumulate. Treat it as a strong direction, not a measured model.

**Applied to the SAT.** The SAT is the ultimate shared, feared, named thing, so the course-in-60-seconds format maps onto it directly: the test, each of its four domains, and the school courses students take for it. Next come the rules every student memorized for the SAT but never understood, each shown transforming. Last come the procedural topics with little visual spectacle.

## SAT in 60 seconds (survey videos)

These are modeled on "Calculus III in 60 seconds" and "All of calculus in 60 seconds". Each one takes a feared, named chunk of the test and shows that it is only a few pictures. Each picture is one of the catalog topics below, compressed to a few seconds. The survey videos and the single-topic videos feed each other.

### S1. All of SAT Math in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** title card "SAT Math / in 60 seconds", with nav "Algebra · Advanced · Data · Geometry".
- **Insight:** The whole math section is four pictures:
  - a line, meaning a constant rate
  - a curve, meaning a rate that changes (parabolas and exponentials)
  - a balance, meaning data and its center
  - a triangle inside a circle, meaning space
  Every question is one of them in disguise.
- **Picture:** One line bends into a parabola, then its points scatter into a data cloud that balances on a fulcrum, then they snap into a triangle inscribed in a circle. The recap stacks all four.
- **On the SAT:** Everything. This is the hub video every other one links back to.

### S2. SAT Algebra in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** "SAT Algebra / in 60 seconds".
- **Insight:**
  - A line is a starting value plus a constant rate.
  - Two lines meeting is the solution.
  - Parallel lines mean no solution, and the same line means infinitely many.
  - Adding the equations rotates a line straight onto the answer.
  - An inequality keeps half of the plane.
- **Picture:** Built from topics 1, 3, 4 and 6 in sequence on one shared grid.
- **On the SAT:** About 35% of the section.

### S3. SAT Advanced Math in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** "SAT Advanced Math / in 60 seconds".
- **Insight:**
  - Lines add, exponentials multiply, parabolas add more each time.
  - A parabola is two lines multiplied, so you can read its zeros directly.
  - The vertex sits halfway between the zeros.
  - The discriminant counts crossings.
- **Picture:** Topics 11, 14, 15 and 18 morphing one curve through all of them.
- **On the SAT:** About 35% of the section, and the hardest questions in the second module.

### S4. SAT Geometry and Trig in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** "SAT Geometry / in 60 seconds".
- **Insight:**
  - Parallel lines make only two angles.
  - Similar triangles scale lengths by k and areas by k².
  - Sine is a ratio that only depends on the angle.
  - The sine of one angle is the cosine of the other.
  - The circle equation is Pythagoras.
- **Picture:** One triangle carrying through the whole video: cut by parallel lines, scaled, measured with trig, and finally spun inside a circle.
- **On the SAT:** About 15% of the section. It's also the domain students feel least prepared for, which gives it a strong hook.

### S5. SAT Data in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** "SAT Data / in 60 seconds".
- **Insight:**
  - Percents multiply.
  - The mean is a balance point and the median is the middle person.
  - Spread is the typical distance from the mean.
  - "Given" shrinks the table.
  - A margin of error is a range, not a miss.
- **Picture:** Topics 31, 34, 35, 38 and 39 on one shared data set.
- **On the SAT:** About 15% of the section.

### S6. Quadratics in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** "Quadratics / in 60 seconds", with nav "factored · vertex · standard".
- **Insight:** There are three forms of one parabola, and each form shows you one thing for free:
  - factored form shows the zeros
  - vertex form shows the turning point
  - standard form shows the y-intercept
  The quadratic formula connects them all.
- **Picture:** One parabola that never moves, with three formulas cycling above it and each form lighting up the feature it reveals.
- **On the SAT:** Quadratics are the single most tested nonlinear topic.

### S7. Every SAT circle question in 60 seconds
- **Hook:** "SAT Circles / in 60 seconds".
- **Insight:**
  - Arcs and sectors are fractions of the circle.
  - Radians measure the angle in radius lengths.
  - The circle equation is Pythagoras, and you complete the square to find the center.
  - A tangent is perpendicular to the radius.
- **Picture:** One circle accumulating each idea, from topics 48 to 52.
- **On the SAT:** Circles show up in a large share of the hardest geometry questions.

## Algebra [about 35%]

Skills: linear equations in one and two variables, linear functions, systems of two linear equations, linear inequalities.

### 1. A line is a promise: same step, every step
- **Hook:** "Every word problem with a line says the same two things."
- **Insight:** A linear function is a starting value plus a constant rate. In y = mx + b, the b is where you start and the m is what happens every single step. Every "a gym charges $40 to join and $25 a month" problem is that sentence.
- **Picture:** A staircase climbing a line, every stair identical. The context labels slide onto b and m.
- **On the SAT:** "What does the 25 represent?" These interpret-the-slope and interpret-the-intercept questions are among the most common on the test.

### 2. Why the slope is the same everywhere
- **Hook:** "Pick any two points on a line. Why do you always get the same slope?"
- **Insight:** Every slope triangle under a line is similar to every other one, so rise over run never changes. Slope works because of similar triangles, which ties algebra to geometry.
- **Picture:** Slope triangles of different sizes sliding along the line, scaling into each other.
- **On the SAT:** Slope from two points or from a table, and checking whether a table is linear.

### 3. No solution means parallel lines
- **Hook:** "3x + 5 = 3x + 7. What's x?"
- **Insight:** Each side of an equation is a line, and the solution is where they cross. When the x terms cancel and leave something false, the lines have the same slope and never meet. When it leaves something true, they're the same line, and every x works.
- **Picture:** Graph the left and right sides. Tilt one until it's parallel and the crossing point runs off to infinity. Slide it onto the other line and the lines merge.
- **On the SAT:** "For what value of k does the equation (or system) have no solution / infinitely many solutions?" This is one of the most reliable hard questions.

### 4. Elimination rotates a line through the answer
- **Hook:** "Why are you allowed to just add two equations together?"
- **Insight:** Adding two equations of lines makes a new line that passes through the same crossing point, because any point on both lines also satisfies their sum. Elimination is choosing the multiple that swings the new line until it's perfectly vertical, so it reads x = something.
- **Picture:** Two lines crossing. A third line pivots through their crossing point as the multiplier changes, and stops at vertical. The answer reads off the axis.
- **On the SAT:** Solving systems, plus "what is the value of x + y" questions, where adding the equations lands straight on the answer.

### 5. Solving an equation is finding where two graphs meet
- **Hook:** "Every equation is secretly two graphs."
- **Insight:** The left side and the right side are each a function of x, and solving means finding the x where their heights match. This turns any equation, even an ugly one, into reading an intersection.
- **Picture:** Left and right sides drawn as curves, with a vertical scanner sliding until both heights agree.
- **On the SAT:** Any equation. It's also exactly what the built-in Desmos calculator does.

### 6. An inequality is half of the plane
- **Hook:** "y > 2x + 1. Where are all the answers?"
- **Insight:** A line splits the plane in two, and an inequality picks a side. A system of inequalities is the overlap. Testing a point means asking which side it's on.
- **Picture:** A line drawn, then the solution side floods with shading. A second inequality shades, and only the overlap stays.
- **On the SAT:** "Which point is a solution to the system?" and graph-matching questions.

### 7. Why multiplying by a negative flips the inequality
- **Hook:** "Why does the sign flip? Nobody tells you."
- **Insight:** Multiplying by -1 reflects the number line in a mirror at zero. Whatever was to the right is now to the left, so the order reverses.
- **Picture:** Two dots, 2 < 5, on a number line. The line flips around 0, the dots land at -2 and -5, and the inequality arrow turns around.
- **On the SAT:** Solving inequalities, and especially compound ones.

### 8. A budget line is a trade-off
- **Hook:** "Adult tickets cost $8, kids' tickets $5, and you spent $200."
- **Insight:** In 8a + 5c = 200, every point is a way to spend exactly $200. The intercepts are "all adults" and "all kids", and the slope is the exchange rate: give up 5 adults to buy 8 kids.
- **Picture:** Coins sliding between two piles while a point walks along the line.
- **On the SAT:** Standard-form context questions about what the intercept or the slope means.

### 9. Perpendicular slopes: rotate the triangle
- **Hook:** "Why is the perpendicular slope the negative reciprocal?"
- **Insight:** Rotating a slope triangle 90 degrees swaps rise and run, and one of them changes sign. So 2/3 becomes -3/2.
- **Picture:** A slope triangle rotating a quarter turn about a point, with its legs relabeling as it turns.
- **On the SAT:** Perpendicular line equations and geometry on the coordinate plane.

## Advanced Math [about 35%]

Skills: equivalent expressions, nonlinear equations and systems, nonlinear functions.

### 10. Squares are stacked odd numbers
- **Hook:** "1, 4, 9, 16. What are you adding each time?"
- **Insight:** You add 3, 5, 7: each new square is the old square plus an L-shaped border. That's why a quadratic's changes grow steadily, and why its second differences are constant.
- **Picture:** A square of tiles growing by L-shaped layers, with the L sizes counting up 3, 5, 7.
- **On the SAT:** Recognizing a quadratic from a table.

### 11. Add, multiply, or square: the three growth families
- **Hook:** "Three functions start at the same place. Only one of them wins."
- **Insight:** Linear adds the same amount each step. Exponential multiplies by the same amount each step. Quadratic adds a growing amount. A table tells you which by its differences or its ratios, and exponential always wins eventually.
- **Picture:** Three curves racing from one point, with their step sizes shown as stacked bars.
- **On the SAT:** "Which model fits?" and linear-vs-exponential context questions, which are very frequent.

### 12. Reading a(1 + r)^t like a sentence
- **Hook:** "1000 times 1.05 to the t over 12. What does any of that mean?"
- **Insight:** Each piece of the formula is a word:
  - a is the start.
  - 1 + r is "multiply by this each time", so 1.05 is +5% and 0.85 is -15%.
  - The exponent counts how many times it happens: t/12 means once every 12 units, and 2^(t/3) doubles every 3.
- **Picture:** A timeline with a multiplier stamping at every tick while the amount bar grows.
- **On the SAT:** "What does 1.05 represent?", "how often does it double?" and choosing an equivalent form of an exponential.

### 13. Completing the square, literally
- **Hook:** "Why is it called completing the square?"
- **Insight:** x² + 6x is a square with two 3-wide strips attached. Fold the strips onto two sides and one corner is missing: a 3 × 3 = 9 piece. Add it and you have (x + 3)². The algebra is just describing the picture.
- **Picture:** An x-by-x square, two strips folding onto it, and the missing corner glowing until it's filled.
- **On the SAT:** Vertex form, maximum and minimum questions, and finding a circle's center and radius.

### 14. A parabola is two lines multiplied
- **Hook:** "Take two lines and multiply them."
- **Insight:** (x - 2)(x + 3) is the line x - 2 times the line x + 3, multiplied height by height. Wherever either line is zero the product is zero, so the parabola crosses exactly at each line's root. Factored form shows you the zeros for free.
- **Picture:** Two lines, with their pointwise product tracing a parabola that touches down exactly where each line crosses the axis.
- **On the SAT:** Zeros and x-intercepts, matching factored forms to graphs, and the zero product property.

### 15. The vertex lives halfway between the roots
- **Hook:** "Where's the top of the arch?"
- **Insight:** A parabola is symmetric, so its vertex sits exactly midway between its two zeros: x = (r + s)/2, which is also x = -b/(2a). Knowing two points at the same height gives you the axis.
- **Picture:** A mirror line dropping into the middle of the arch, with the two halves folding onto each other.
- **On the SAT:** Vertex and axis questions, and "f(1) = f(7), so the vertex is at x = 4".

### 16. Why the shift goes the wrong way
- **Hook:** "f(x - 3) moves right. Why does minus mean right?"
- **Insight:** Changes inside the parentheses act on the input. To get the same output as before, x has to be 3 bigger, so the whole graph lands 3 to the right. Changes outside the parentheses act on the output and go the way they look.
- **Picture:** An input dial and an output dial on a function machine, with the graph sliding as each dial turns.
- **On the SAT:** Graph transformation questions and reading vertex form.

### 17. The quadratic formula is a midpoint plus or minus a distance
- **Hook:** "The scariest formula in high school, read in two parts."
- **Insight:** -b/(2a) is the axis of symmetry, the midpoint. The ± part is how far each root sits from that midpoint. The formula just says: start in the middle, step the same distance both ways.
- **Picture:** The formula splitting into two highlighted parts. A dot lands on the axis, then two arrows of equal length reach out to the roots.
- **On the SAT:** Solving quadratics that don't factor, and understanding solutions written as a ± √b.

### 18. The discriminant counts crossings
- **Hook:** "One number tells you how many solutions, before you solve."
- **Insight:** b² - 4ac is the thing under the square root. Positive means two crossings, zero means the parabola just touches, and negative means it never reaches the axis. It's the "distance" from topic 17 becoming imaginary.
- **Picture:** A parabola sliding up through the axis, with the counter going 2, then 1, then 0 as b² - 4ac goes positive, zero, negative.
- **On the SAT:** "For what value of c does the equation have exactly one real solution?" and "how many solutions?" questions, which are frequent and hard.

### 19. Roots add to -b, multiply to c
- **Hook:** "Find the sum of the solutions, without solving."
- **Insight:** If x² + bx + c = (x - r)(x - s), expanding gives r + s = -b and rs = c. Some of the hardest questions on the test become one line.
- **Picture:** A rectangle with sides r and s whose area is c, and two segments r and s joining into one bar of length -b.
- **On the SAT:** "What is the sum of the solutions?" questions, a known hard-module shortcut.

### 20. Where a line meets a parabola
- **Hook:** "A line and a parabola. How many times can they meet?"
- **Insight:** Substituting the line into the parabola leaves one quadratic, and its discriminant decides 0, 1 or 2 meetings. The single-meeting case is the line just grazing, a tangent.
- **Picture:** A line sweeping across a parabola: two crossings, then one kiss, then none.
- **On the SAT:** Nonlinear systems and tangency "value of k" questions.

### 21. Equivalent means the same graph
- **Hook:** "Two expressions that look nothing alike. Are they the same?"
- **Insight:** Equivalent expressions give the same output for every x, so their graphs lie exactly on top of each other. That's why you can match coefficients: if ax² + 6x equals 3x² + bx for all x, then a = 3 and b = 6.
- **Picture:** Two different-looking expressions drawn as graphs. One morphs until it lands on the other and they become a single curve.
- **On the SAT:** "Which expression is equivalent?" and "true for all x, find a" questions, which are very common.

### 22. The difference of squares, cut and moved
- **Hook:** "Why does a² - b² factor so cleanly?"
- **Insight:** Cut a b × b square out of an a × a square. Slice off the leftover strip and move it, and the L shape becomes a rectangle measuring (a + b) by (a - b).
- **Picture:** A square with a corner removed, a strip sliding into place, and the rectangle forming.
- **On the SAT:** Factoring and simplifying rational expressions.

### 23. A zero is a factor
- **Hook:** "p(3) = 0. What does that tell you?"
- **Insight:** If a polynomial is zero at 3, then (x - 3) is one of its factors. That's the same picture as topic 14: the graph touches down where a factor vanishes. Dividing by (x - r) leaves a remainder of exactly p(r).
- **Picture:** A polynomial crossing at three points, each one lighting up its matching factor in the formula.
- **On the SAT:** "Which must be a factor of p?", reading zeros from tables, and remainder questions.

### 24. Bounce or cross
- **Hook:** "Why does this graph touch the axis and turn back?"
- **Insight:** A squared factor such as (x - 2)² never changes sign, so the graph touches the axis and bounces back. A single factor changes sign, so the graph crosses.
- **Picture:** Side by side, (x - 2) crossing the axis and (x - 2)² bouncing off it, each with its sign shown above.
- **On the SAT:** Matching polynomial graphs to equations.

### 25. Exponent rules are just counting
- **Hook:** "Why is x to the zero equal to 1?"
- **Insight:** x³ · x² is five x's multiplied together, so exponents add. Going down the pattern x³, x², x¹ divides by x each step, which lands on x⁰ = 1 and x⁻¹ = 1/x. And x^(1/2) · x^(1/2) = x means x^(1/2) is the square root.
- **Picture:** Rows of x tiles merging, then a staircase of powers descending past 1 into fractions.
- **On the SAT:** Equivalent expressions with rational and negative exponents.

### 26. Squaring both sides can lie
- **Hook:** "You solved it perfectly. One answer is still wrong."
- **Insight:** Squaring treats √x = 2 and √x = -2 as the same equation, so it can invent a solution that never existed. The fake solution belongs to the mirror-image curve.
- **Picture:** y = √x and a line meeting once. After squaring, the mirror branch appears and makes a second, fake intersection, which then fades out.
- **On the SAT:** Radical equations and "which solution is extraneous?"

### 27. Absolute value is distance
- **Hook:** "|x - 3| = 5. Why two answers?"
- **Insight:** |x - 3| is how far x is from 3. Being 5 away from 3 happens in two places, 8 and -2. An inequality like |x - 3| < 5 is everything within 5.
- **Picture:** A number line with a center at 3 and a ruler swinging 5 units each way.
- **On the SAT:** Absolute value equations and inequalities, and tolerance problems.

### 28. f(3) is just a height
- **Hook:** "Function notation, in one picture."
- **Insight:** f(3) is the height of the graph above x = 3. Solving f(x) = 3 is the opposite question: find where the graph is 3 high. Composition f(g(x)) is two of those lookups chained together.
- **Picture:** A vertical probe at x = 3 reading off a height, then a horizontal probe at height 3 finding x.
- **On the SAT:** Reading graphs and tables, and composition questions.

## Problem-Solving and Data Analysis [about 15%]

Skills: ratios, rates and units; percentages; one-variable data; two-variable data and scatterplots; probability and conditional probability; inference from samples and margin of error; evaluating statistical claims.

### 29. A ratio is a slope through the origin
- **Hook:** "Double the recipe. What else doubles?"
- **Insight:** Proportional quantities sit on a straight line through (0, 0), and the ratio is its slope. That's why scaling works: every point on the line has the same ratio.
- **Picture:** Recipe points falling on a line from the origin, with the slope triangle labeled with the ratio.
- **On the SAT:** Proportional relationships and scaling.

### 30. Unit conversion is multiplying by 1
- **Hook:** "How to never mess up a unit conversion again."
- **Insight:** 60 minutes / 1 hour equals 1, so multiplying by it changes nothing but the units. Units cancel exactly like variables do, and you pick the fraction that cancels the unit you don't want.
- **Picture:** A chain of fractions where matching units cross out like terms, leaving the unit you want.
- **On the SAT:** Rates, unit conversions and density, which are very frequent.

### 31. Up 20%, down 20% is not where you started
- **Hook:** "A price goes up 20%, then down 20%. Is it back where it started?"
- **Insight:** No. Percent changes multiply, they don't add: 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, so you lose 4%. The 20% off is taken from a bigger number.
- **Picture:** A bar stretching by 20%, then shrinking by 20% of the new, longer bar, and falling short of the starting line.
- **On the SAT:** Successive percent changes, reverse percents (the price after 20% off is $80, so the original is 80 / 0.8), and percent-of questions.

### 32. "Percent of" versus "percent change"
- **Hook:** "From 40 to 50. Is that a 20% increase or a 25% increase?"
- **Insight:** Percent change is always the change divided by the original. 10/40 = 25% going up, but 10/50 = 20% going back down. The starting point is the whole.
- **Picture:** Two bars, with the "whole" highlighted switching depending on the direction.
- **On the SAT:** Percentage questions, which are among the most common on the test.

### 33. A weighted average leans toward the crowd
- **Hook:** "One class averages 90, another 70. Is the combined average 80?"
- **Insight:** Only if the classes are the same size. The combined mean leans toward the bigger group, like a balance with more weight on one side.
- **Picture:** Two piles of different sizes on a beam, with the fulcrum sliding toward the heavier pile.
- **On the SAT:** Combined means and "what score do you need" problems.

### 34. The mean is a balance point
- **Hook:** "Why does one billionaire wreck the average but not the median?"
- **Insight:** The mean is where the data balances, so a far-away point has huge leverage. The median just counts to the middle and doesn't care how far away the outlier is.
- **Picture:** A dot plot on a seesaw. An outlier slides out and the balance point (the mean) chases it, while the median marker stays put.
- **On the SAT:** "What happens to the mean and median if the outlier is removed?", which is frequent.

### 35. Standard deviation is typical distance
- **Hook:** "Two classes, same average. Which one is more consistent?"
- **Insight:** Standard deviation measures how far a typical point sits from the mean. Adding the same amount to every value doesn't change it. Multiplying every value scales it.
- **Picture:** Two dot plots with the same center, distance lines drawn from each dot to the mean, one set short and one long.
- **On the SAT:** "Which data set has the larger standard deviation?" questions, judged by eye.

### 36. A line of best fit is a prediction machine
- **Hook:** "What does the slope of a scatterplot line actually tell you?"
- **Insight:** The slope is the predicted change in y for each one-unit increase in x. A residual is the vertical gap between what happened and what the line predicted.
- **Picture:** Scatter points, a line settling through them, and residual sticks dropping from points to the line.
- **On the SAT:** Interpreting the slope and intercept of a model, and predictions and residuals.

### 37. Probability is a fraction of the possibilities
- **Hook:** "What is probability, really?"
- **Insight:** Probability is the favorable outcomes as a share of all equally likely outcomes. Drawn as an area, the favorable part covers that share of the whole.
- **Picture:** A grid of outcomes with the favorable cells filled in.
- **On the SAT:** Basic probability.

### 38. "Given" shrinks the universe
- **Hook:** "One word changes the whole probability."
- **Insight:** "Given that the student is a junior" means you only look at juniors. The rest of the table disappears, and the probability is out of that row alone.
- **Picture:** A two-way table where every row except one fades away and the denominator changes in front of you.
- **On the SAT:** Conditional probability from two-way tables, which appears on nearly every test.

### 39. Margin of error is a range, not a miss
- **Hook:** "The poll says 52%, plus or minus 3. Who's winning?"
- **Insight:** A sample gives an estimate with a plausible range around it. Larger samples tighten the range, and the claims the data supports are the ones that stay true across the whole range.
- **Picture:** Many samples' estimates scattering around the truth, clustering tighter as the sample size grows.
- **On the SAT:** Inference statements: which conclusion the data supports.

### 40. Random sampling versus random assignment
- **Hook:** "Can this study say coffee *causes* better grades?"
- **Insight:** Random sampling lets you generalize to the population. Random assignment lets you claim cause. You need the right kind of randomness for the claim being made.
- **Picture:** A 2 × 2 grid: sampled or not, assigned or not, with each cell showing which claim it allows.
- **On the SAT:** Evaluating statistical claims, usually one question per test.

## Geometry and Trigonometry [about 15%]

Skills: area and volume; lines, angles and triangles; right triangles and trigonometry; circles.

### 41. Parallel lines make only two angles
- **Hook:** "Eight angles. How many different sizes?"
- **Insight:** Two. When a line crosses two parallel lines, every angle is either the small one or its partner that makes 180°. All the vocabulary (alternate interior and so on) collapses into this.
- **Picture:** A transversal crossing two parallel lines, with the eight angles colored into two groups.
- **On the SAT:** Angle-chasing questions, which are frequent.

### 42. Why a triangle's angles add to 180
- **Hook:** "Any triangle. Rip off the corners."
- **Insight:** The three corners line up into a straight line. Also, the exterior angle equals the sum of the two far interior angles, which is a shortcut that skips a step.
- **Picture:** The three corners tearing off and sliding together into a straight line.
- **On the SAT:** Angle measures and exterior angles.

### 43. Every triangle is half a rectangle
- **Hook:** "Why one half base times height, for every triangle?"
- **Insight:** Shearing slides the top of a triangle sideways without changing its area. Every triangle is half of a parallelogram with the same base and height.
- **Picture:** A triangle's top vertex sliding along a line while the area readout stays fixed, then a doubled copy completing a parallelogram.
- **On the SAT:** Area problems, including composite shapes.

### 44. Double the side, quadruple the area
- **Hook:** "Double a cube's side. How much more paint, and how much more water?"
- **Insight:** Scaling by k multiplies lengths by k, areas by k² and volumes by k³. Double the side means 4 times the area and 8 times the volume.
- **Picture:** One square tiling into four copies, one cube stacking into eight.
- **On the SAT:** Similar figures and scaling questions where the area or volume ratio is asked, which are frequent and a common trap.

### 45. Special right triangles are half of simple shapes
- **Hook:** "Why does a 30-60-90 triangle have sides 1, √3, 2?"
- **Insight:** It's half of an equilateral triangle. A 45-45-90 triangle is half of a square. Pythagoras finishes each one.
- **Picture:** An equilateral triangle splitting down the middle, and a square splitting along its diagonal.
- **On the SAT:** Special right triangles. They're on the reference sheet, but this is why they work.

### 46. SOH-CAH-TOA is just similar triangles
- **Hook:** "Why does sine only depend on the angle?"
- **Insight:** Every right triangle with a 30° angle is a scaled copy of every other one, so opposite/hypotenuse is the same number for all of them. Sine is the name of that fixed ratio.
- **Picture:** Right triangles of different sizes, all with the same angle, scaling into each other while the ratio readout never moves.
- **On the SAT:** Trig ratios in right triangles.

### 47. Sine of an angle is cosine of the other one
- **Hook:** "If sin x = cos y, what's x + y?"
- **Insight:** In a right triangle the two acute angles swap roles: the side opposite one is adjacent to the other. So sin(x) = cos(90° - x). "Co-sine" literally means the sine of the complement.
- **Picture:** One right triangle with the viewpoint switching between its two acute angles, and the side labels swapping.
- **On the SAT:** The sin/cos complement question, which appears almost every test.

### 48. Radians: measure the angle with the radius
- **Hook:** "What is a radian, actually?"
- **Insight:** Take the radius, bend it around the circle, and the angle it covers is 1 radian. Halfway around the circle is π radii, so 180° = π.
- **Picture:** A radius peeling off and wrapping along the circle, three and a bit times to reach halfway.
- **On the SAT:** Converting between degrees and radians, and the unit circle.

### 49. Arcs and sectors are fractions of the circle
- **Hook:** "Arc length without memorizing a formula."
- **Insight:** A 60° slice is 60/360 = 1/6 of the circle, so it gets 1/6 of the circumference and 1/6 of the area. Every arc and sector formula is that fraction.
- **Picture:** A pizza slice opening up, with the fraction feeding both the crust length and the slice area.
- **On the SAT:** Arc length and sector area, which are frequent.

### 50. The circle equation is Pythagoras
- **Hook:** "(x - h)² + (y - k)² = r². Where did that come from?"
- **Insight:** Every point on a circle is r away from the center. The horizontal and vertical gaps form a right triangle with hypotenuse r. The circle equation is just Pythagoras, written for every point at once.
- **Picture:** A point moving around a circle, dragging its right triangle to the center, with legs labeled x - h and y - k.
- **On the SAT:** Circle equations. The harder ones need completing the square (topic 13) to find the center and radius.

### 51. The inscribed angle is half the central angle
- **Hook:** "Why is any angle in a semicircle exactly 90 degrees?"
- **Insight:** An angle drawn from the edge of a circle is half the central angle on the same arc. A semicircle's central angle is 180°, so the angle from the edge is 90° wherever the vertex sits.
- **Picture:** A vertex sliding around the circle's edge while the angle readout stays locked.
- **On the SAT:** Circle angle questions.

### 52. A tangent is perpendicular to the radius
- **Hook:** "Where a line just kisses a circle, what's the angle?"
- **Insight:** The tangent point is the closest point on the line to the center, and the shortest path to a line is perpendicular. So the radius meets the tangent at 90°, which makes a right triangle for Pythagoras.
- **Picture:** A line sliding toward a circle until it touches, with the radius snapping in at a right angle.
- **On the SAT:** Tangent and circle triangle questions.

### 53. Three pyramids make a cube
- **Hook:** "Why does a pyramid get one third?"
- **Insight:** Three identical pyramids fit together exactly into a cube. Any pyramid or cone is one third of the prism or cylinder around it. Slide a stack of thin slices sideways and the volume doesn't change, so slanted shapes follow the same rule.
- **Picture:** Three pyramids assembling into a cube. Then a stack of coins slanting while the volume readout holds steady.
- **On the SAT:** Volume of pyramids, cones and composite solids.

## Cross-cutting ideas

These are bigger ideas that tie several domains together. They work as series openers or closers.

- **Every graph is a list of solutions.** Points on a graph are the answers to its equation, and solving is reading intersections. Links topics 3, 5, 6 and 20.
- **Similar triangles run half the test.** Slope, trig and scaling are all the same idea. Links topics 2, 44 and 46.
- **Area is the hidden algebra.** Completing the square, the difference of squares, roots as a rectangle, squares as odd numbers. Links topics 10, 13, 19 and 22.
- **Multiplying is the hidden percent.** Percent change, growth factors and exponential models. Links topics 12, 31 and 32.
