However, the fractions and later prime-count labels are small relative to the title. Their readability in the full recording should not be assumed from the sheet's thumbnail size.

### Object inventory and correspondence

- One large initial curve, quiet coordinate axes, marked points, rational-coordinate labels and a secant-like line. Blue marks the active point rather than coloring every rational point.
- Three repeated comparison rows with rank 0/1/2 labels, finite/one-direction/two-direction point arrangements and example equations. Visible equation examples include `y²=x³+1`, `y²=x³-x+1` and `y²=x³+17`.
- A finite residue grid and a prime/count readout for the same displayed curve, then a schematic L-function view.
- Three function panels with a shared `s=1` guide, zero markers and row-specific zero-order descriptions.
- A seven-position series/progression motif and a source-logo card. These are navigation/branding assets, not mathematical evidence.

The strongest editorial device is the matched row: the left-hand categorical structure survives while the right-hand representation changes. The weakest bridge is the transformation from prime counts to an analytic graph. A visually smooth transition cannot substitute for its missing definition. Likewise, constructing points from a line is not a full account of generators, torsion and rank. The final relationship is explicitly framed as conjectural. It would be a serious overclaim to call this a proof of the named conjecture, or even a full derivation of each displayed function from the counts.

### Cognitive burden, payoff and SAT transfer

The clip moves quickly through several prerequisite layers: rational coordinates, intersections on an elliptic curve, generation from seeds, rank, modular counting, an analytic function and a zero's order. The repeated rows reduce search burden, but cannot remove those conceptual dependencies. A learner might remember the rank/zero pairing without understanding what either object is. For a discovery overview that can be an honest orientation payoff; it would fail a promise to teach the underlying theory completely.

**Concrete SAT adaptation:** use three repeated rows to compare `y=(x-3)²+c` at `c=1,0,-4`, all against `y=0`. Keep the vertex's horizontal location fixed. The rows show no real intercepts, one at x=3 and two at x=1,5. Earn the cases from `(x-3)²=-c`: a square cannot be negative; it is zero in one way; and a positive square value has two sign branches. The payoff is not a memorized category label but a reason for each case. A nearby transfer asks for the number of real roots when `c=-9` and yields two.

**Do not copy:** the number of advanced vocabulary changes, tiny example formulas, a smooth visual bridge that stands in for an unshown mathematical definition, prize framing as an educational reason to care, or the source's series/brand assets. Carry over the comparison grammar and active-row emphasis. Keep our SAT premises and proof visible.

### Object inventory, logic and limits

The inventory is unusually economical: a point-centered grid, an E axis, horizontal levels, a vacuum baseline, a first-excitation line, one blue vertical gap marker and the symbol `Δ>0`. A denser mesh and a handful of filled cells serve as a computational-looking comparison. The teaser uses a seven-position navigation motif and a small curve drawing from the following topic.

The most transferable equation correspondence is direct: the bracket's positive height becomes a positive-gap symbol. It is meaningful because the baseline and next level remain visible while the notation arrives. The least established correspondence is from a wavy two-dimensional mesh to a rigorous four-dimensional quantum theory. The drawing communicates an idea; it does not construct that theory or prove its spectrum has a gap. The source's final existence-and-gap phrasing is evidence that it presents a research problem rather than a completed proof. No current status or award eligibility was independently verified here.

The visual density drops at the key inference. Dense lattice -> sparse levels -> two endpoints and a bracket is a useful way to make an abstract absence inspectable. The risk is that a learner mistakes a drawn blank interval for evidence that nature or the mathematical model must contain it. If the explanation promises only an intuition for a named problem, the scope is defensible. If it promises proof, the animation cannot deliver it.

### Concrete SAT transfer and what not to copy

**SAT adaptation:** compare `x>2.4` for real x with the same inequality when x must be an integer. On a fixed number line, show the continuous eligible interval first; it has values arbitrarily close to 2.4 and no smallest eligible real number. Then reveal the integer restriction and retain only ticks 3,4,5,..., with 3 as the smallest eligible integer. Put the domain condition beside the prompt throughout. The new blank interval between the threshold and first eligible integer has a mathematical reason, not merely a drawn gap. A transfer at `x>7`, integer x, yields smallest value 8; `x≥7` yields 7.

**Borrow:** establish two endpoints, let empty space carry information, use one bracket to connect geometry and notation, clear dense support material before the decisive inference, and put the next action after the payoff.

**Do not copy:** a wavy mesh as generic “advanced math” decoration, a fabricated numerical energy scale, an unproved physical assertion presented as a derivation, an unstated discrete-domain assumption, or research-problem grandeur attached to elementary SAT content. The integer example is an analogy of presentation, not an analogy claiming quantum energy is an ordinary number-line rounding rule.

### Apparatus, burden and scope

The clip uses one formula throughout much of the process, two named mathematical roles (`z` in the rule and `c` on the parameter marker), a current point, fading path history, axes/origin, a membership-like backdrop, a discrete grid and the final contour renderer. The background silhouette is not merely decorative in the local stage: it places a selected parameter relative to the larger object. That relationship is the intellectual content the zoom-only imitation would lose.

It also demands hidden prerequisites. Complex-plane position, repeated squaring, a fixed parameter versus a changing iterate, infinite iteration and boundedness are different ideas. The formula can look simple while the conceptual load remains high. A viewer may understand “repeat a simple rule and get intricate structure” without being able to execute or classify the iteration. That is a discovery experience, not proof of the Mandelbrot set's detailed properties.

A useful distinction for our standard is **computing examples versus certifying general behavior**. The finite orbit, dot grid and increasingly detailed image do not on their own prove membership for every parameter or self-similarity claims at every scale. A SAT clip should make the exact finite computation it promises checkable. It should not inherit “infinite complexity” language merely because a camera zoom is beautiful.

### Concrete SAT transfer and what not to copy

**SAT adaptation:** keep a single function machine `f(x)=2x+1` on the page and ask for `f(f(2))`. Track the same value through two passes: 2 enters, 5 leaves; 5 then becomes the next input, producing 11. Use two numbered iteration markers and an actual return path from output to input. Only after those values are inspectable, expand `f(f(x))=2(2x+1)+1=4x+3`. A nearby transfer is `f(f(3))=15`. This is a new finite function-composition lesson, not a claim that real linear iteration models a fractal boundary.

**Borrow:** rule persistence, explicit roles for parameter/input/output, fading trace history, one controlled change for the contrast, local-process-to-global-picture structure and an earned zoom after the essential inference.

**Do not copy:** a lengthy zoom as proof, insufficiently explained symbols, fake zooms between unrelated assets, tiny many-point fields at the expense of the active object, purple as an automatic new series color, or the unsupported conclusion that fractals must use a dark aesthetic. Zoom only when scale is part of the idea or a clearly labeled visual reward after a completed bounded lesson.

## What these four recordings add to the standards

The common visual grammar is a **persistent apparatus with a changing explanatory role**. Curve points become ranked families; a field becomes levels with a gap; one iteration rule becomes local paths then a global set; an accumulated region becomes a local increment then a quotient. Each offers something beyond moving labels. An agent should identify the exact role change it needs before building an impressive transition.

Three concrete output decisions follow:

1. **Give every new symbol a visible referent.** FTC is strongest when the width and height are labeled before multiplication. Yang-Mills is strongest when both energy endpoints exist before the gap symbol. For SAT, a denominator, side length or excluded case deserves the same treatment.
2. **Reuse the comparison frame, then earn the correspondence.** BSD's three rows and shared guide make comparison efficient; Mandelbrot's persistent rule and fading trace make repeated steps legible. Retaining the frame helps attention, but a morph cannot prove the relationship. Our algebra must supply any missing general step.
3. **Judge the payoff by the promised format.** An advanced discovery overview can honestly leave a viewer with an intuition and an open problem. A SAT skill lesson promises a reason the student can use on a nearby problem. Borrowing the creator's beauty while omitting the transferable inference fails our output bar, even if the resulting video looks similar.

The corresponding failure patterns are visible too: too many prerequisite jumps, tiny technical labels, an unexplained link between different mathematical spaces, finite samples mistaken for proof, and a zoom mistaken for an argument. These are concrete risks to our teaching and review criteria, not assertions that the references failed publicly. There are no matched performance metrics for these four captures and no measured learning outcomes. The defensible conclusion is that they supply specific craft decisions and scope warnings, not demonstrated causes of views.
